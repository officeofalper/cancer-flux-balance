Try these experiments

  1. Compensation. Load "GLS drug 50%". Growth falls only a little, because pyruvate carboxylase refills the TCA cycle from glucose. Now switch pyruvate carboxylase off.
  2. Context decides. Load "FASN drug, lipid-poor", then raise fatty acids from outside. The drug stops working once the cell can import enough lipid. Do the same with the PHGDH drug and serine.
  3. Oxygen matters. Raise the LDHA drug in normoxia: almost nothing happens. Load "Hypoxia" and try again.
  4. Two drugs. Combine a GLS and a PHGDH drug. Is the effect bigger than the stronger drug alone?

How the model works

Flux balance analysis. 40 lumped reactions link nutrients to building blocks. Linear programming finds the reaction rates v that maximise growth, given:

S · v = 0             (steady state)
lb ≤ v ≤ ub           (supply, drugs)
Σ cost_i · v_i ≤ 1    (space budget)

A drug at x% caps its enzyme at (1 − x) times the rate it had without the drug. Among equally good solutions, the one with the smallest total flux is shown. The solver in this page gives the same answers as the Python script.

Where this comes from

Based on my published review "Metabolic Reprogramming in Cancer Cells" (#94): aerobic glycolysis, de novo lipogenesis (FASN), glutaminolysis and serine/one-carbon metabolism are linked, and single-pathway drugs are limited by metabolic compensation (Sections 5.4, 5.5 and 8).

The reactions, costs, nutrient levels and drug rule are my own modelling choices, not results from the paper. The model has no gene regulation, no enzyme kinetics and no thermodynamics; it shows which routes the network allows, not what a real cell does.
